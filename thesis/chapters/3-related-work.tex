\chapter{Related Work}

\section{Extending Data Contracts and Semantic Layers for Agent Consumption}
The closest work to this study is that of \citeonline{bandari2025aiready}, who propose a
specification for data products meant to be consumed by agents. It extends data contracts
with entity and grain declarations, join paths with cardinality, governed metric
definitions, synonym bindings, value domains, quality and freshness signals, usage
constraints, and an interface exposed through MCP. They evaluate a GPT-4o agent on 120
business questions over four data products under three conditions: the raw schema, the
schema with a conventional contract, and the full specification. Execution accuracy rises
from 41.9\% to 53.9\% and to 79.7\%, and answers that execute but are semantically wrong
fall from 41.1\% to 11.9\% of the runs. The design is the same incremental comparison used
here. The differences are that their richest condition is a richer contract, not an
ontology, that a single agent model is used, and that no significance test is reported.

\citeonline{rumiantsau2026semantic} compare, in a paired design, three frontier models
answering 100 questions over a retail dataset with the schema alone or with the schema plus
a hand-written semantic document of 4~KB. The document adds 17 to 23 percentage points of
accuracy, from 45.5--50.5\% to 67.7--68.7\%. \todo{Read the full text for the definitions of

\section{Ontologies and Knowledge Graphs over Enterprise Data}
\citeonline{sequeda2023benchmark} built a benchmark of 43 questions over a 13-table subset
of an insurance data model and compared GPT-4 answering with SQL over the database against
SPARQL over a knowledge graph of the same data with an ontology. Accuracy, measured as
execution accuracy averaged over runs, was 16.7\% for SQL and 54.2\% for the knowledge
graph; for questions over more than four tables SQL scored zero. The authors list among
the limitations the use of a single model and of one-to-one mappings between tables and
ontology terms. \citeonline{allemang2024ontologies} extended the approach with a
deterministic check of the generated query against the ontology and a repair loop of up to
three cycles, after which an unrepaired query is returned as unknown. Accuracy rose to
72.55\%, with 8\% of unknown answers and an error rate of 19.44\%.

These studies compare a database without a semantic layer against one with an ontology. The
present case starts from a system that already has a rich semantic layer in its contracts,
which is the situation in which the benefit of an ontology is least obvious.

\citeonline{fitch2026natural} study how the representation of an ontology affects query
generation by locally served models, with 21 competency questions over a research archive.
Among eight representations, accuracy of the generated SPARQL ranged from 100\% for the
default representation to 10\% for one that replaces names by abstract identifiers. This
supports the finding of this case that the vocabulary carried by the ontology matters more
than its structure.

\section{Tool and Dataset Selection in MCP}
\citeonline{mudunuri2026semantic} retrieve MCP tools by embedding similarity instead of
exposing the whole catalogue. On 140 queries over 121 tools from five servers they report a
hit rate of 97.1\% at $K=3$, a mean reciprocal rank of 0.91 and a 99.6\% reduction in
tool-related tokens. \citeonline{mo2025livemcpbench} evaluate agents on 95 tasks over 70
servers and 527 tools, with success judged by a language model; most models succeed in 30
to 50\% of the tasks, and in the trajectories analysed half of the failures are retrieval
errors. Both use the measures adopted here for routing (hit rate, reciprocal rank) and for
cost (tokens).
